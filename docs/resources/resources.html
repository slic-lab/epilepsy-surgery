Epilepsy surgery options

There are many types of epilepsy surgery. Some remove or treat a small part of the brain that causes seizures. Others use a device to change brain signals. The best choice depends on where seizures start and which parts of the brain need to stay safe.

This page gives general information. It cannot tell you which treatment is right for you. An epilepsy team can explain the possible benefits and risks for your situation.

1

Open resection

Removing the area where seizures start

What it is: A surgeon opens the skull and removes the brain area that causes seizures. A lobectomy removes part of one brain lobe.

When it may be used: It may be an option when seizures start in one clear area that doctors can remove safely.

Main goal: To stop seizures or reduce how often they happen.

Learn more from the Epilepsy Foundation →
2

Laser ablation (LITT)

Treating a small area with heat from a laser

What it is: A thin laser probe goes into the brain. MRI helps the team find the right spot. Heat from the laser treats the tissue that causes seizures.

When it may be used: It may help when seizures come from a small, well-defined area.

Main goal: To treat the seizure source through a small opening.

Learn more from the Epilepsy Foundation →
3

Stereotactic radiosurgery

Treating the seizure area with focused radiation

What it is: Radiation beams are aimed at a small target in the brain. The skull does not need to be opened. Gamma Knife and CyberKnife are two systems that can do this.

When it may be used: It may be an option when doctors can target the seizure area very closely.

Main goal: To treat the tissue causing seizures while limiting radiation to nearby areas.

Learn more from the Epilepsy Foundation →
4

Deep brain stimulation (DBS)

Sending regular electrical signals to the brain

What it is: A surgeon places thin wires in a deep part of the brain. A small device under the skin of the chest sends signals through the wires.

When it may be used: It may be an option when seizures are hard to control and the seizure area cannot be removed safely.

Main goal: To reduce seizures by changing brain signals.

Learn more from the Epilepsy Foundation →
5

Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS)

Sending electrical signals through a nerve in the neck

What it is: A small device is placed under the skin of the chest. A wire joins it to a nerve in the neck called the vagus nerve. The device sends small signals at set times.

When it may be used: It may be an option when medicines do not control seizures and other surgery is not a good choice.

Main goal: To reduce how often seizures happen or how severe they are.

Learn more from the Epilepsy Foundation →
6

Radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RF-TC)

Treating a small seizure area with heat

What it is: A doctor uses a thin wire in the brain to heat a small area linked to seizures. Sometimes the same wires used to find where seizures start can give this treatment.

When it may be used: It may be an option when doctors can find and safely reach a small seizure area.

Main goal: To reduce seizures by treating the tissue where they begin.

7

Responsive neurostimulation (RNS)

A device that detects and responds to seizure activity

What it is: A device watches brain signals. When it finds unusual activity, it sends a small electrical pulse to the seizure area.

When it may be used: It may help some people with focal seizures when the seizure area cannot be removed safely or other surgery has not worked.

Main goal: To reduce seizures by responding when abnormal brain activity begins.

Learn more from the Epilepsy Foundation →
8

MRI-guided focused ultrasound

Using focused sound waves to heat a small brain target

What it is: Focused ultrasound sends sound waves to one small spot in the brain. The waves can heat and treat a small amount of tissue. MRI helps the team guide the treatment.

For epilepsy: Doctors are still studying this treatment for epilepsy. It is not a standard option for most people.

Main goal: Researchers are studying whether it can treat seizure areas without open surgery.

How is an option chosen?

Before surgery, an epilepsy team uses tests such as EEG and MRI. These tests help show where seizures start and what nearby brain areas do. The team looks at the possible benefits and risks. The right choice is different for each person.
